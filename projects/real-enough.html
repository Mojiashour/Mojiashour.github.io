Real Enough for What? A Review of Human Response Correspondence Between VR and Real Environments

Mojtaba Ashour, Armin Mostafavi, Jialin Wu, and Saleh Kalantari

  • Ecological Validity
  • Systematic Review
  • Virtual-Real Comparison
  • Spatial Cognition
  • Human-Building Interaction
Under review

Although Virtual Reality (VR) is increasingly used to study human behavior, the degree to which findings can support inferences about real-world experience remains uncertain. To address this question, we conducted a systematic review of 65 empirical studies directly comparing matched virtual and real indoor environments. The review synthesized correspondence across four domains of human response: perception, cognition, affect, and behavior and interaction, while examining environmental and methodological factors including spatial scale, visual realism, presence, interaction, locomotion, and virtual-environment creation methods.

The findings show that correspondence is partial and outcome-dependent. Twenty studies reported predominantly similar outcomes, 31 mixed outcomes, and 14 predominantly different outcomes. Correspondence was more common for categorical judgments, self-report ratings, and structured cognitive tasks, while spatial judgments, memory and transfer, and physiological responses more often diverged. We distinguish between pattern, magnitude, and process correspondence, demonstrating that similar outcomes do not necessarily reflect equivalent effects or underlying behaviors. The resulting framework positions ecological validity not as an inherent property of VR, but as dependent on the alignment between the intended real-world inference, environmental representation, interaction design, task demands, and evaluation methods.
